A curb ramp is a constructed pedestrian slope bridging the height difference between a
raised sidewalk and the roadway gutter, located at intersection corners, marked or unmarked
crosswalks, mid-block crossings, and medians or islands. Look for three geometries: (1)
perpendicular ramps: a sloped run cut through the curb, flanked by triangular flared sides
or returned curbs; (2) parallel ramps: the sidewalk slopes down from one or both sides to a
level landing at street level; (3) blended transitions: a broad corner area lowered nearly
flush with the street. Many ramps have a high-contrast detectable warning surface of
truncated domes (yellow, red, white, or gray; rectangular or curved) at the street edge;
older ramps are plain or grooved concrete with no domes—detect both. Count a curb ramp
wherever the curb visibly drops to street level at a pedestrian crossing point, even if it
is old, cracked, steep, narrow, missing detectable warnings, partly hidden, or far away.
Box the full ramp structure (sloped run, flares, and warning surface) from where it leaves
sidewalk level down to the gutter. A parallel ramp or a blended transition gets one box. If
a corner has two ramps separated by raised curb or grass, box each; a single apex ramp
serving both crossings gets one box. Exclude driveway and alley aprons (vehicle curb cuts,
usually wider and mid-block, leading to garages, alleys, or parking, with no domes and the
sidewalk staying level across them), full-height curbs with no lowered section, and storm
drain inlets.
