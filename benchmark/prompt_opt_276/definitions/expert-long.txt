A curb ramp is a short, sloped section of pedestrian walkway that cuts through or descends
from a raised curb, so a person using a wheelchair, walker, stroller, or cane can move
between sidewalk level and street level without a step. Following ADA and U.S. Access Board
(PROWAG) guidance, curb ramps are placed where a pedestrian route meets a street crossing:
at intersection corners, at marked or unmarked crosswalks, at mid-block crossings, and on
medians or refuge islands.

Visual cues in a street-level photo:
- A break in the curb line where the vertical curb face tapers down until it is flush, or
  nearly flush, with the gutter.
- A sloped surface, usually concrete, about 4–6 ft wide (roughly pedestrian-walkway width,
  narrower than a car lane). It is often lighter or newer than the surrounding sidewalk and
  may have grooved or scored lines.
- Triangular flared sides ("wings"), or vertical returned curbs, on either side of the
  sloped surface.
- A detectable warning surface at the street edge: a panel of raised truncated domes about
  2 ft deep spanning the ramp width, usually yellow, red, white, or dark gray. It is strong
  evidence of a curb ramp, but many older ramps lack it.
- Usually faces a crosswalk, an opposite corner, or a mid-block crossing point.

Common forms:
- Perpendicular: runs straight from the sidewalk to the street at a right angle to the
  curb. A corner often has two, one for each crossing.
- Diagonal (apex): a single ramp at the middle of the corner that serves both crossings.
- Parallel: the sidewalk itself slopes down along the curb from one or both sides to a low
  landing at street level.
- Blended transition (depressed corner): a broad area of the corner lowered almost flush
  with the street, usually edged with detectable warnings.

Count a curb ramp if the curb visibly drops to street level at a pedestrian crossing
point, even if it is old, cracked, steep, narrow, missing detectable warnings, partly
hidden, or far away.

Box extent: draw one box per ramp. Include the sloped surface, its flares, and its
detectable warning panel, from where it leaves sidewalk level to where it meets the gutter.
Two distinct ramps at the same corner get two boxes. A parallel ramp (both sloped runs plus
the landing) gets one box, and so does a blended transition.

Not curb ramps:
- Driveway aprons and alley entrances. These are curb cuts for vehicles leading across the
  sidewalk to a garage, parking lot, yard, or alley—usually wider, mid-block, and where the
  sidewalk often stays level across them (do not box vehicle driveways even if the sidewalk
  dips or has warning domes).
- A full-height curb with no lowered section (this is a missing curb ramp, so do not box
  it).
- Building entrance ramps, ramps with handrails, and loading docks.
- Raised crosswalks and speed tables, where the street rises to sidewalk level instead.
- Tactile domes at rail or transit platforms that are not at a street curb.
- Curb paint, stop bars, storm grates, and utility covers.
